How droplet size is measured

On the Size Determination page, droplets are found with a Hough Circle Transform, which fits circles to edge arcs in the image rather than requiring a full closed silhouette — this makes it reasonably tolerant of partial overlap between droplets. Before detection, each image's grayscale contrast is automatically stretched to fill the full brightness range, which makes detection far more robust to images with inconsistent brightness (e.g. uneven illumination) without needing separate settings per image.

Detected circles are also passed through a duplicate-suppression pass: lowering the "min distance between centers" setting to catch legitimately close/touching droplets can also let the detector report two slightly different circles fit to the same blurry droplet edge. Near-perfectly-coincident detections (tight relative to droplet size) are merged, while genuinely distinct droplets — even touching or overlapping ones — are left alone, since their centers are still separated by roughly their combined radii, not a few pixels of fit jitter.

Diameter in real-world units follows directly from the calibrated scale (µm per pixel) applied to each detected circle's pixel diameter.

How the contact angle is calculated

For two droplets whose outlines overlap in the image, the Contact Angle page estimates the geometric contact angle between them — correcting for the fact that droplets of different sizes sit at different heights when resting on the same substrate.

The setup

Each droplet is treated as an undeformed sphere resting tangent to a shared horizontal substrate (e.g. droplets settled on a glass slide under gravity), imaged from directly above. Because a sphere resting on a plane touches it at exactly one point, a droplet's center sits at height equal to its own radius above that plane:

z = r

Two droplets of different radii therefore have centers at different heights purely because of their size difference. A single top-down image only shows the lateral (x–y) separation between two droplets, not this height difference — so naively measuring the angle from the 2D image alone would be wrong. The diagram below shows the correction.

Side view (not to scale). The camera looks straight down, so it directly measures d_xy — the horizontal separation — and each droplet's own radius. The vertical offset Δz follows from the tangent-sphere assumption, giving the true 3D distance D via the Pythagorean relationship.

The equations

With apparent (image-plane) radii r₁, r₂ and apparent center-to-center distance d_xy, all already measured in real units via the calibrated scale:

Δz = |r1 − r2|
D = d_xy² + Δz²

A pair only counts as truly touching if the reconstructed spheres actually reach each other:

D ≤ r1 + r2

Otherwise the droplets only appear to overlap in the 2D image — an artifact of the height difference — and no angle is reported ("not in contact").

When they are in contact, the angle follows from the standard two-sphere intersection geometry (law of cosines on the triangle formed by the two centers and their point of contact), with the sign chosen so the angle grows with increasing overlap/adhesion rather than shrinking (0° = a bare graze, matching the conventional direction for a droplet-droplet adhesion angle):

θ = arccos(D² − r1² − r2²2r1r2)

Assumptions and limitations

  • Each droplet is assumed to remain a perfect, undeformed sphere — not flattened by gravity or by pressing against its neighbor. Reasonable for small, surface-tension-dominated droplets; less so for larger or heavily-loaded ones.
  • The apparent 2D radius is assumed to equal the true equatorial radius, which holds for a roughly telecentric/low-distortion imaging setup — standard for droplet size measurement, and the same assumption the diameter measurements elsewhere in this tool already rely on.
  • Every droplet in the frame is assumed to rest on the same substrate height.
  • This is a single-image, single-focal-plane model. It has no way to detect real deviations from a sphere. Relaxing that would require z-stack imaging to directly measure each droplet's height rather than inferring it from radius.
